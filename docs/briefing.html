Communication range and the LPAC swarm policy

A published graph-neural-network policy for robot swarms gets worse when its robots can talk to more of each other. This page explains the problem, everything we measured, what it means, and what each of us does next.

Proposal Tue 20 Oct, 11:59 pm PT Milestone 10 Nov Final report 10 Dec Repository github.com/loicpoisson/lpac-communication-range (private)

Read this first

  • The phenomenon. LPAC is a published policy that coordinates 32 robots through a GNN. Trained with a 128 m radio range, it is the best controller we tested at 128 and 256 m. Give it a longer range and its cost triples by 1024 m, where it becomes worse than simple non-learned algorithms. This happens in all 11 test environments.
  • It is not a quirk of one file. We retrained LPAC ourselves with the authors' recipe, three times with different seeds. All three collapse the same way.
  • The cause is not the graph. LPAC sees its neighbors twice: as edges in the graph given to the GNN, and as dots in a small image (the "neighbor map") given to a CNN. Fixing the graph changes nothing. Drawing the image at a fixed 256 m scale, with the same weights, removes the collapse entirely.
  • Training is affordable. A full training run at the paper's scale takes 2 h 35 on one free Stanford FarmShare GPU.
  • The project. Show where neighbor geometry should live in a swarm policy. The graph ML contribution is a variant that drops the neighbor image and carries relative positions on the graph's edges, then a test of whether it transfers across communication ranges.

Section 1

The task: coverage control

A team of robots must spread over a square area to watch it. Some places matter more than others: this is the importance density field (IDF), for example a fire, a pollution plume or crops to inspect. The robots do not know the field in advance. Each robot only sees what is under its own sensor and builds a local map as it moves. A robot can only talk to robots within its communication range. There is no central computer: every robot decides its own next move.

How performance is measured

The coverage cost sums, over the whole field, the importance of each point times its squared distance to the nearest robot. Lower is better. We report the cost averaged over the 600 steps of an episode, divided by the same quantity for the clairvoyant algorithm in the same environment. A value of 2.0 means "twice the clairvoyant's cost". This is the metric of the paper's Figures 15, 19 and 20.

The four controllers we compare
ControllerWhat it knowsRole in this study
Clairvoyant CVTThe whole field and all robot positions.Reference: cost ratio = 1. Impossible to deploy. Also the teacher that LPAC imitates during training.
Centralized CVTAll robots' local maps, sent to a server.Non-learned baseline. Does not depend on the communication range.
Decentralized CVTIts own map and its neighbors' positions.Non-learned baseline. Uses neighbors only inside a 256 m local window.
LPACIts own maps and messages from robots within range.The learned policy we study.

Section 2

The LPAC model

LPAC (Learnable Perception-Action-Communication; Agarwal, Muthukrishnan, Gosrich, Kumar and Ribeiro, IEEE Transactions on Robotics 2025) runs the same small network on every robot. The released configuration, which we read in the code, is the following.

Components, as configured in the released files
PartInputOutputDetails
CNN4 maps of 32 × 3232 numbers3 convolution layers, kernel 3. Channels: local importance, neighbor map x, neighbor map y, obstacles.
Concatenation32 + robot position (x, y)34 numbersMatches the paper's d₀ = 34.
GNN34 per robot + graph256 per robot5 layers of torch_geometric.nn.TAGConv, K = 3, ReLU after each. Binary edges: edge_weight is built but not passed.
MLP + linear2562Layers 256 → 32 → 32, then 32 → 2, rescaled by the mean and std of the training actions.

What TAGConv computes

For node features X, adjacency A and degree matrix D, one TAGConv layer computes

X' = Σ_{k=0..K} ( D^-1/2 · A · D^-1/2 )^k · X · W_k

This is the symmetrically normalized adjacency used by GCN, applied up to K = 3 times inside each layer, so up to 15 propagation steps across the 5 layers. It is not a plain sum over neighbors. The normalization divides by the square roots of the degrees, which keeps message sizes bounded when a robot has many neighbors.

What the neighbor map is, exactly

For robot i and communication range R, the map is a 32 × 32 grid covering the square from −R to +R around the robot, in two channels. Each neighbor j within range lands in one cell and adds its relative position to it:

cell(i, j)  = round( (p_j − p_i) · 32 / (2R) + 15.5 )     one cell = 2R / 32 metres
value(i, j) = (p_j − p_i) / R                            channel x and channel y
map[i, cell] += value(i, j)                              robots in the same cell are summed

Two consequences matter. First, the cell size grows with the range: 8 m at 128 m, 16 m at 256 m, 64 m at 1024 m. Second, values are divided by R, so the same neighbor 50 m away writes 0.39 at 128 m but 0.05 at 1024 m. The two local perception maps do not change with the range; only the neighbor map does.

Section 3

What the communication range changes

Raising the range does three things at once. All three were measured on the published model's own trajectories, 32 robots in a 1024 × 1024 m world.

Range (m)Neighbors per robotIsolated robotsNeighbor-map cellValue of a neighbor at 50 m
1281.424 %8 m0.39

  1. The graph gets denser: from 1.4 to 25 neighbors per robot out of 31 possible. At 1024 m the graph is close to complete.
  2. The neighbor map gets coarser and fuller: more robots are drawn, in bigger cells, with smaller values.
  3. Both are far from anything the model saw during training at 128 m.

The question of the project is which of these changes breaks the policy.

Section 4

The paper's claim and what we can test

The performance of the LPAC-K3 model improves until a communication range of 512 m and then decreases. This is counterintuitive. […] Investigating these hypotheses and improving the performance of the LPAC model with a larger communication range is an interesting direction for future work. LPAC paper, Section VI-H and Figure 20

In Figure 20 the authors train a separate model for each range and evaluate each at its own range, on 100 environments. They propose three explanations and test none:

HypothesisAuthors' wording, summarizedOur evidence so far
H1 neighbor mapThe fixed 32 × 32 map sums several robots per cell at large ranges and loses information.Supported in our transfer setting (F4 to F6). Not yet separated from plain distribution shift.
H2 aggregationThe GNN aggregates by summation, which is lossy.Does not describe the code: TAGConv uses normalized adjacency.
H3 dense graphThe graph becomes nearly complete and GNNs work better on sparse graphs.Secondary. Capping the graph has no effect when the map is wrong, a real but small effect when the map is empty (F4, F6).

What we cannot do. Only one trained model is public: model_k3_1024_state_dict.pt, byte-identical in releases v1.1.0 to v1.3.1, trained at 128 m according to its parameter file. We checked the releases, the other branches and tags, the authors' pages, their GitHub accounts and Hugging Face. The per-range models of Figure 20 are not available.

So our main results are transfer results: one model trained at one range, run at others. They do not reproduce Figure 20. We asked the authors' co-author for the other checkpoints (section 10). We can now train per-range models ourselves (F9), which is how we will connect to Figure 20.

How much noise to expect. Moving the 32 initial positions by 1 m changes an episode's cost by about 7 %: the standard deviation within an environment is 0.35 for the published model at 1024 m and 0.09 once repaired. On the mean of 11 environments, differences below about 0.03 (repaired model) or 0.1 (published model) are not interpretable. Between three retrained seeds, the mean moves by 0.01 at 128 m and 0.26 at 1024 m, so comparing two trained variants requires about 0.05 at 256 m and 0.5 at 1024 m, or more seeds.

F3 · DONE

No over-smoothing

Over-smoothing (lecture 5) means that repeated message passing makes all nodes' representations identical. We measured the effective rank of the 32 robots' representations after each TAGConv layer: the exponential of the entropy of their normalized singular values, between 1 (all identical) and 32.

Shows

The rank drops about 30 % with range in layers 2 to 4 and never approaches 1, while the cost triples. Representations stay diverse even with 25 neighbors.

That the graph plays no role at all; F4 and F6 measure that directly. We use effective rank rather than Dirichlet energy because Zhang et al. (ICLR 2026) show Dirichlet-type measures fail for shallow networks.

F7 · DONE

Noise floor and reproducibility

  • Each environment rerun 5 times with initial positions moved by a 1 m Gaussian: about 7 % of the cost (section 5).
  • The clairvoyant itself varies by 0.26 % under the same perturbation, so the normalization is stable.
  • FarmShare AMD nodes gave different numbers (up to 5.6 % per environment) because of different convolution kernels. Those 64 jobs were discarded and rerun on Intel nodes; Slurm now requests Intel.
  • The simulator draws new environments from a hardware random source and cannot be seeded: generated environments must be saved to disk to be reused.

F8 · DONE

Training LPAC is affordable

We used the authors' scripts unchanged: data_generation.py runs the clairvoyant in random environments and records, every 5 steps, all robots' maps, positions and target actions; train_lpac.py fits the network to those actions (mean squared error, Adam, learning rate 10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻³, batch 100). One "sample" is the state of all 32 robots at one time step; the paper's 100,000 samples are about 250 episodes.

Section 7

What we can and cannot claim

We can claim

  • LPAC trained at one range fails badly at larger ranges, for the published model and for our own.
  • For the published model, the failure enters through the rasterized neighbor map, not through the GNN's graph.
  • A fixed-scale map restores the best performance at every range, without retraining.
  • The GNN path (normalized TAGConv) tolerates a graph 20 times denser than in training.

We cannot claim yet

  • Anything about the paper's Figure 20, where each model is trained at its own range.
  • That 256 m is the right fixed scale in general (chosen on the test environments).
  • Why the map fails: information loss versus distribution shift.
  • That a policy can benefit from a longer range. Our fix only stops the damage.
  • Anything beyond 32 robots in a 1024 m world.

The project question

Where should neighbor geometry live in a swarm policy: in an image read by a CNN, or in the graph read by a GNN? Our results say the image is where LPAC breaks when the range changes. The graph ML step is to remove the image and carry relative positions on the edges, then test whether that policy transfers across ranges and, ideally, benefits from more communication.

Section 8

Related work and novelty

We found no work that runs LPAC, or a comparable swarm policy, at a larger communication range than it was trained with. Search: the Google Scholar list of papers citing LPAC (25 entries), targeted web searches, the authors' pages, their GitHub accounts and Hugging Face. Three theses on the citing list are still unread: Gosrich (2024), Owerko (2025) and F. Yang (2026).

WorkWhat it showsWhat it leaves open
LPAC (T-RO 2025)Transfer to larger worlds and teams at a fixed 128 m range; per-range models in Figure 20.No evaluation outside the training range; the anomaly is unexplained.
Muthusamy et al. (2024), with C. KanatsoulisRobots talk to their k = 3 nearest neighbors; the policy improves when agent density rises at test time. Their code tests checkpoints while varying the radius.A different task and a k-nearest graph; no radius graph, no neighbor image.
MAST (2025), same groupA transformer replacing the GNN; larger attention windows generalize worse to larger teams.Windows varied at training time, not between training and test.
GNN generalizationOutside robotics: performance degrades when test degrees differ from training degrees; the normalized adjacency's spectrum depends on the graph.No control policy, no inference-time fix.

So "a model fails out of distribution" is not new. Our contribution is to show, measure and locate the failure in a published robotics policy, where it triples the cost, to show that the graph path is the robust one, and to build a policy that does not need the fragile path.

The graph-native variant (N4), concretely

  • Input to the CNN: only the two perception maps (local importance, obstacles). The library already supports this with UseCommMaps = false.
  • Edges: the same communication graph; each edge carries the relative position (p_j − p_i), in metres or divided by a fixed constant, never by the range.
  • Message passing: replace TAGConv with a PyG layer that reads edge attributes, for example GATv2Conv(..., edge_dim=2) or TransformerConv(..., edge_dim=2); keep 5 layers and width 256 so the comparison is fair.
  • Training: the authors' data recipe and hyperparameters, 100,000 samples, best-validation checkpoint, on a FarmShare GPU at night (about 3 h per run).
  • Evaluation: the same 5 ranges, the 11 released and the 40 new environments, 3 seeds.

What counts as success

  • Minimum: at its training range, as good as LPAC retrained at 128 m (within 0.05 at 256 m).
  • Transfer: at 1024 m, better than LPAC retrained at 256 m (2.31) by more than the 0.5 seed threshold, or flat across ranges.
  • Strong result: at or below 1.71 at 1024 m, the inference-time fix, without needing a fix; or better with more range.
  • If it fails: the project still stands as a diagnosis with a zero-training fix, and the failure itself is informative. Say so plainly.

Mistakes we corrected

Our planning made several wrong assumptions. They are listed so that nobody repeats them in the proposal.

We first thoughtWhat is true
The GNN sums its neighbors, so the GIN expressivity argument (lecture 6) applies.It is TAGConv with normalized adjacency.
Over-smoothing explains the collapse.Effective rank does not collapse; the neighbor map is the cause.
Use knn_graph to cap the degree.That links robots out of radio range; we keep the k nearest within range.
Measure over-smoothing with Dirichlet energy.Use effective rank (Zhang et al., ICLR 2026).
No trained model is published.One is, attached to the GitHub releases.
Training data would take hundreds of terabytes.Maps are stored at 32 × 32: about 37 GB for 100,000 samples.
The graph is weighted.Edge weights are computed but not passed to the GNN.

Section 13

Glossary

Coverage control
Placing a team of robots so their sensors watch the important parts of an area.
IDF (importance density field)
How important each point of the area is. Unknown to the robots; discovered by moving.
Coverage cost
Importance-weighted squared distance from each point to its nearest robot, summed over the area. Lower is better.
Clairvoyant, centralized, decentralized CVT
Voronoi-based (Lloyd) algorithms with full, server-level and neighbor-level information. The clairvoyant is the reference and the imitation teacher.
Communication range
Distance within which two robots can exchange messages. It defines the graph and the scale of the neighbor map.
Neighbor map
Two 32 × 32 image channels showing, around a robot, the relative positions of robots within range. Read by the CNN.
Communication graph
Robots as nodes, an edge between robots within range. Read by the GNN.
TAGConv
PyG graph convolution that mixes powers 0 to K of the normalized adjacency.
Transfer setting
A model trained at one range and run at another. All our results on the published model are in this setting.
G0, G1
Graph given to the GNN: all robots within range (G0, as published) or capped to the k nearest within range (G1).
C0 to C4
Neighbor map given to the CNN: as published (C0), k nearest only (C1), drawn at the 128 m scale (C2), at the 256 m scale (C3), empty (C4).
H1, H2, H3
The paper's three hypotheses: neighbor map loses information, lossy aggregation, graph too dense.
N1 to N5
Our next experiments, section 9.
Effective rank
How many dimensions the robots' representations really use: exp of the entropy of normalized singular values. Collapses to 1 under over-smoothing.
Over-smoothing
Repeated message passing making all node representations identical. Not observed here.
Imitation learning
Training a policy to reproduce an expert's actions from the partial information it will have when deployed.
Seed
The random initialization of a training run. Comparing variants needs several.
Paired difference
Difference between two conditions computed environment by environment, then averaged; removes the large differences between environments.
